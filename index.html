Why

Every clock you own
is set by someone else.

Your phone is set by a carrier. Your laptop by a time server. The hour on your wall moves twice a year because a government says so. Every one of them can be changed by somebody who is not you.

What we made

One that can't be.

Since January 2009 the Bitcoin network has kept its own time — a block roughly every ten minutes, paid for in work, adjusted by nothing but difficulty. It is the only clock in the world that nobody sets. This is the first one you can wear.

Honesty

It never shows a number
it cannot defend.

Height is fetched, never inferred. The moment a fetch fails the face says so — EST +36 BLK +/-6 — because the size of its ignorance is more useful than a confident number that is quietly wrong.

Scarcity, counted

Twenty‑one million,
worked out on your wrist.

Every coin issued and every coin left, to the hundredth — derived on the watch from the block height alone. No API is involved, because the height is the calendar.

What it refuses

Everything the cloud promises.
Nothing it takes.

No app. No account. No custodian. No telemetry, no subscription, and no opinion about your step count. It reads a public chain and shows you what it found.

The wallet

It can be paid.
It cannot spend.

A watch-only zpub derives addresses and holds no key, so losing the watch loses no coins. Hold DOWN and the balance goes behind dashes while the receive code keeps working.

An afternoon on the sand, and a chain that carried on regardless.

The plain face

And a mode that
shows nothing at all.

Four digits, a date, and no height, no price, no strip — nothing to tell anyone what you are wearing. Tap once and the chain comes back for a single glance.

Time travel

Go to any block.
Come back.

The subsidy, the supply, the epoch and the halving are all functions of height, so none of them need a network. The watch will take you to block 2,279,279, show you the face as it will read in 2051, and label every projection as one.

One alarm

It will ring once,
and never again.

At block 1,000,000 — roughly May 2027 — the watch clears the screen, buzzes ten times and tells you what just happened. Then it writes to flash so it can never do it twice. Every one of these watches is waiting for the same moment.

The object

Machined, numbered,
and dated by the chain.

CNC aluminium, bead blasted and anodised, thirty grams. The caseback carries your number and the block that confirmed your payment — a date nobody can reissue or fake.

The last thing it does

When it runs out, it leaves
the last block it saw.

E-paper holds its image with the power off, so the watch does not freeze mid-face or brown out halfway through a refresh. It puts up one screen and stops, showing the height it last knew. The chain carried on without it.

The record

Twenty‑five faults.
All of them published.

The calendar was nine years wrong. The balance was missing my change. The watch that would not charge was reading a full cell as empty. Every one is written down with its fix, because a claim you cannot check is only advertising.
